Privacy Policy

Version 2026-09-10.2. Effective on the date of first store release.

The short version

Card Genie runs on your device. We do not run a server of our own. What exists elsewhere is your account at the sign-in service and, under it, a copy of your wallet — the cards you carry by name, your offers, your merchant reports and your preferences — in a database, Google Cloud Firestore, that only your account can read. That copy is what lets your wallet follow you to a new phone.

The questions you ask, your usage counts and your welcome answers stay on your phone. You sign in — with Apple, Google or an email address — so that your wallet can be saved under your account, and nothing about your wallet is readable by anyone but you.

We never ask for a card number, and there is no field in the app for one.

We do not sell your information, share it for advertising, build a profile of you, or track you across other apps and websites. Those are not policy positions we could quietly reverse; there is no code in the app capable of any of them.

Anonymous usage counts, together with the answers you pick in the welcome questions on first launch, reach us if you finish those questions, and Diagnostics turns them off. They carry no name, no email address and no account identifier, and they are the only thing that reaches us that is not under your account.

The rest of this document is the detail behind those five paragraphs, written out at the length the subject deserves. Nothing later contradicts anything here.

Who this policy is from, and what it covers

Card Genie is an independent application. This policy is written by the person who makes it and describes the behaviour of the Card Genie mobile application on iOS and Android, and of the browser build of the same application.

It covers what the application does on your device and what it sends over a network. It does not cover, and cannot cover, what Apple, Google, or any other company does with information you give them directly — including when you sign in with an account they hold. Those relationships are between you and them, under their own policies, and are described here only so you can see where the boundary is.

If any statement in this document is wrong, that is a defect and should be reported like one, at github.com/shahp-08/card-genie.

What the app stores on your device

All of this is kept in the application’s private storage — AsyncStorage on iOS and Android, localStorage in the browser build. The wallet, your account profile and your preferences in the list below are also saved under your account, as the section “Your wallet under your account” describes; everything else in the list stays on the device and is transmitted nowhere.

  • Your wallet: which cards you added, by product name (for example “Chase Sapphire Preferred”); whether each is active; any nickname you gave it; when you told us the account was opened; and how much you have told the app you have spent against a bonus cap.
  • Your account, if you made one: the name you typed, an emoji if you picked one, and — if you signed in — which provider vouched for you, the opaque account identifier they returned, and the display name and email address they shared. Apple’s “Hide My Email” relay address is a normal and expected value here.
  • Your preferences: how you value points (cash, transfer, or a custom cents-per-point figure), and which valuation the app ranks by.
  • Your merchant reports: when the app did not recognise a store and you told it what kind of place it is, the store name you typed and the category you chose, with the date.
  • Your offers: bank offers you typed in — merchant, value, minimum spend, expiry, notes — and whether and how much you marked used.
  • Recent questions: the last 20 things you asked, with the answer’s card name and value, so the For You screen can show them.
  • Diagnostics, if enabled: a short log of application errors with amounts, quoted text, merchant names, email addresses and long digit runs removed before they are written, and a buffer of usage events containing counts and catalog identifiers only.
  • A cached copy of the kill-switch list described below, which is public information identical for every user and says nothing about you.
  • Your welcome answers: which options you picked in the short questionnaire shown on first launch — how you heard about the app, why you downloaded it, roughly how many cards you carry, how you choose a card today, and an age band. Only the option identifiers are stored, never free text, because there is nowhere in that questionnaire to type any.
  • A random installation identifier, generated on the device, whose only job is to label anonymous usage statistics if you choose to send them. It is not derived from your device, your account or anything about you, it is shown in Diagnostics, and Delete everything discards it.
  • A note of where this device stands against the copy under your account: a revision number, the time of the last change, and whether a change is waiting to be sent. It contains no token and nothing about your cards.

What the app never stores

This list is exhaustive and structural rather than aspirational. There is no screen that asks for any of it, no field in any stored record that could hold it, and the writer that produces both the backup file and the copy under your account refuses to write anything whose field name resembles one.

  • Card numbers, in full or in part. The app identifies cards by product name and could not use a number if you gave it one.
  • Expiry dates, security codes, CVV or CVC values, or PINs.
  • Bank usernames, passwords, or any credential for any financial institution.
  • The password for an email sign-in. It is sent once, over an encrypted connection, to the sign-in service that checks it, and there is no field in the application in which to keep it.
  • Statements, transactions, balances, or any data from your bank. The app has no bank connection and no mechanism to obtain one.
  • An Apple or Google token, or any sign-in token, in the application’s ordinary storage. The record we keep of a sign-in has fields for the provider, an opaque identifier, a name, an email address and a date, and nothing else; anything further a provider returns is discarded before it reaches storage. The one credential the application holds — a refresh token for the session that saves your wallet under your account — lives in the phone’s keychain, as “Signing in” describes, and nowhere else.
  • Your location, at any precision. The app requests no location permission.
  • Your contacts, photos, camera, microphone, calendar, health data or files.
  • Advertising identifiers, including the iOS Identifier for Advertisers. The app does not request tracking permission because it has nothing to do with it.
  • Biometric data of any kind.

Signing in

The application asks you to sign in with Apple, Google or an email address before you use it. Signing in is what your wallet is saved under: the copy described in “Your wallet under your account” is stored against the account identifier the sign-in service returns, and can be read by no other account. In a build with no sign-in provider configured, the application lets you in without one and keeps the wallet on the device alone.

When you choose to sign in, we ask Apple, Google or — if you pick email — Firebase Authentication, a sign-in service operated by Google LLC, to confirm who you are. They hand back an identifier that is unique to you and to this application and is meaningless anywhere else, along with whatever name and email address you chose to share at that moment. We keep those on your device.

Apple and Google each return an identity token. The application hands it, once, to Firebase Authentication, which checks it and returns a session for the same person: an id token that lasts an hour, held in memory, and a refresh token that renews it. The refresh token is written to exactly one place, the phone’s keychain (the iOS Keychain, or Android’s encrypted preferences), readable only while the phone is unlocked and never carried to another device by a backup. It is never written to the application’s ordinary storage, never included in a backup file, and never sent anywhere except to Firebase to renew the session. Signing out deletes it; so does deleting your account. In the Google flow an access token is also used for a single request for your name, email address and identifier, and discarded. The Apple authorization code is not used. A build without the sync service keeps no session at all.

Signing in with an email address and password works like this. The name, address and password you type are sent, once and over an encrypted connection, to Firebase Authentication, which creates or checks the account and returns an account identifier. The application keeps the identifier, the name and the address on your device: the password is never written anywhere by the application. The session the service returns is used while the “verify your email” step is on screen — to put your name on the account, to ask the service whether you have followed the link in the verification email, and to send that email again — and, once the address is verified, becomes the session described above, whose refresh token goes to the keychain and nowhere else. An email sign-in does not complete until the address is verified. Firebase Authentication keeps its own record of the account — the name, the address, a salted hash of the password, and the dates it was created and last used — under Google’s privacy policy. “Forgot password?” asks the service to send you a reset email; the application never sees the new password either.

We do not run a server that sees your sign-in. Your sign-in is a relationship between you and Apple, Google or Firebase Authentication, governed by their privacy policies; the database that holds your wallet checks the session the sign-in service issued and nothing else. Apple’s “Hide My Email” is fully supported: if you use it, we receive a relay address and never learn your real one, and the application works identically either way.

Signing out removes the identifier and the provider’s name and email from your device and the session from the keychain, while keeping your cards, your typed name and your preferences on the device and the copy under your account; you are asked to sign in again the next time you open the application. It is not the same as deleting, which is described below.

Two devices signed into the same account share the copy of the wallet under it, and nothing else: the later change wins, whole, and the questions asked on one are never seen by the other.

Your wallet under your account

When you are signed in, the application saves a copy of your wallet under your account: the cards you added, by product name; whether each is active; nicknames; when you told us an account was opened; what you have told the app you spent against a bonus cap; the offers you typed in and what you marked used; your merchant reports; how you value points; and your profile name and emoji. It is the same document as a backup file, and like a backup file it contains no card number and no credential, because the application has none to write; an automated check refuses to write anything whose field name resembles one.

It is stored in Google Cloud Firestore, a database service operated by Google LLC, in the United States, in the same Firebase project as the sign-in service, under the account identifier the sign-in returned. The database’s access rules allow a request to read, write or delete the document under an identifier only when it carries a valid session for that identifier; there is no path by which one account reads another’s, and we do not run a server of our own that reads them. The rules are published in the repository beside the application, in the file firebase/firestore.rules.

The copy is read when you sign in and once at each launch, and written shortly after each change you make. When this device and the copy disagree, the later change wins and replaces the other whole; the application never merges two wallets, for the reason given under backups. If the service cannot be reached, the change waits on the device and the Account screen says so. The copy is used for nothing but giving your wallet back to you: no analysis, no aggregation across accounts, no sharing.

The questions you ask, your usage counts, your welcome answers, your recent questions and the diagnostics log are not part of the copy and never leave the device except as described under usage statistics.

A build without the sync service configured saves nothing under the account, and the sign-in screen says so.

Backups you make

You can export a backup file at any time, whether or not you have an account. It contains your wallet, your preferences and your profile name — which is to say, the list of which financial products you hold. It contains no card numbers and no credentials: the application has no card number to write, and the one credential it holds, the sync session, is in the keychain where the backup writer cannot reach it and would be refused by name if it could.

The file goes wherever you send it. We never receive it and cannot recover one you lose. Once it leaves the application it is governed by whatever service you put it on, not by this policy.

The file is not encrypted. That is a deliberate choice rather than an oversight: a passphrase box producing cryptography we could not stand behind would be worse than none, and the places people realistically keep such a file — Files, iCloud Drive, Google Drive, a mail account — encrypt it at rest already. What we owe you instead is an accurate sentence about what is in it, which is the paragraph above. If that list is sensitive to you, keep the file somewhere you consider secure.

Restoring a backup replaces what is on the device rather than merging with it, and the application tells you so before it does anything.

What the app sends over the network

This is the complete list. The application makes no other outbound request of its own, and every entry here is checked against the code by an automated test that fails if a module can make a request this section does not describe.

  • Kill-switch list (automatic, only in a build configured with a feed address): once at launch the application may fetch a small public JSON file listing cards we have switched off — for example because we found a rate to be wrong. The request carries no identifier for you, no wallet and no question; it is the same file for everyone, fetched the way any static file is. What comes back can only ever stop the application using a card, never change a rate or a number.
  • Store lookup (optional, and off unless the build includes a search key): when you name a store the application does not recognise, it may send the store name you typed — nothing else — to Brave Search to find out how that store is categorised. Not your amount, not your cards, not your question, not any identifier for you. The result is always shown to you as unconfirmed and you are asked to confirm it. Brave’s own privacy policy governs that request.
  • Signing in with Apple (only when you tap it): handled by Apple’s own system sheet. The application receives the result; it does not send Apple anything about you beyond the fact that this application is asking. In a build with the sync service, the identity token Apple returns is then sent once to Firebase Authentication, at identitytoolkit.googleapis.com, which checks it and returns the session described under “Signing in”.
  • Signing in with Google (only when you tap it): opens Google’s sign-in page in a browser, then makes two requests to Google — one to exchange the authorization code for a token, one to read your name, email address and account identifier. In a build with the sync service, the identity token from the first is then sent once to Firebase Authentication in the same way. No information about your cards, your questions or your usage is included in any of them.
  • Signing in with an email address (only when you use it): up to five requests to Firebase Authentication, at identitytoolkit.googleapis.com — to create or check the account, to put your name on it, to send a verification or password-reset email, to ask whether the address has been verified, and to delete the account when you ask for that. Each carries the address and, where the request needs it, the name, the password or a short-lived token for that one request. Nothing about your cards, your questions or your usage is included in any of them.
  • Saving your wallet under your account (only when signed in, in a build with the sync service): requests to Cloud Firestore, at firestore.googleapis.com, to read the copy under your account at launch and after a sign-in, to write it shortly after each change, and to delete it when you delete everything. Each carries the session’s id token and, when writing, the wallet document described under “Your wallet under your account”. Nothing about the questions you ask, your usage or your device is included.
  • Renewing the session (only when signed in, in a build with the sync service): a request to securetoken.googleapis.com carrying the refresh token, which returns a new id token, roughly once an hour while the application is in use. Nothing else is included.
  • Usage statistics (only in a build with a collector key; on from the moment you finish the welcome questions on first launch, and Diagnostics turns them off or back on): the application periodically posts a snapshot of aggregated counts — how many questions were asked, answered and marked helpful, how far you got through setup, how many days you have opened it, and which welcome answers you picked — to PostHog, an analytics service, labelled with the random installation identifier described above. Never individual events, never what you typed, which store, how much, a card number, your name, your email address or your account identifier.

The application loads no remote fonts, images, scripts, stylesheets or advertising. It contains no analytics SDK, no advertising SDK, no attribution SDK, no crash-reporting service and no tag manager: the usage snapshot above is a plain request the application writes itself, which is why this policy can say exactly what is in it. It does not contact a language-model provider: the parser and the sentences it writes are rules and templates running offline on your device.

What we do not do

Stated plainly, because most policies bury these and because in this case each is a statement about code that does not exist rather than a promise about behaviour we could change quietly.

  • We do not sell your personal information, and never have. We have no mechanism to do so and no relationship with any data broker or advertiser. The one analytics service we use, described above, receives aggregated counts and questionnaire answers under a random identifier, and nothing that identifies you.
  • We do not share your personal information for cross-context behavioural advertising, and we do not serve advertising of any kind.
  • We do not build a profile of you, score you, or make any automated decision that produces a legal or similarly significant effect. The application ranks your own cards for a purchase you describe, on your device, and that is the whole of it.
  • We do not track you across other applications or websites, and the application requests no permission that would allow it.
  • We do not use cookies, web beacons, tracking pixels, fingerprinting, or any similar technology. The browser build uses localStorage to hold your own wallet on your own device and nothing else.
  • We do not buy information about you from anyone.
  • We do not have a mailing list and will not email you. The only email an address of yours receives on our account is the verification or password-reset message you ask the sign-in service for.

Legal bases for processing, for readers in the European Economic Area and the United Kingdom

Under the General Data Protection Regulation, processing needs a lawful basis. Because the application processes your information on your own device, and the copy under your account is readable by that account alone and used for nothing else, our role as a controller is narrow. Where the framework applies:

  • Storing your wallet, preferences and history on your device: necessary for the performance of the service you asked for, under Article 6(1)(b). Without it the application cannot do the one thing it exists to do.
  • Signing in, keeping the identifier a provider returns, your account at Firebase Authentication, and the copy of your wallet under it in Cloud Firestore: necessary to provide the application as designed, under Article 6(1)(b), since the application asks for a sign-in before use and the copy is what carries your wallet to a new phone. The identifier and the session are removed by signing out; the copy and the account are deleted from inside the application.
  • The store lookup, in builds where it is enabled: our legitimate interest in identifying how a merchant is categorised, under Article 6(1)(f), balanced by sending only the store name and no identifier for you.
  • Usage statistics and welcome answers, in builds with a collector: our legitimate interest in understanding how the application is found and used, under Article 6(1)(f), balanced by the fact that they carry no name, address or account identifier — only counts and answers under a random installation label — and by the switch in Diagnostics, which turns them off.

We do not process special categories of personal data under Article 9, and we do not carry out automated decision-making within the meaning of Article 22.

How long information is kept

Information stays on your device until you remove it, the application is uninstalled, or your device’s storage is cleared. The copy of your wallet under your account stays in Cloud Firestore until you delete everything from the Account screen, which removes it at once; it is not kept after that, and there is no retention schedule beyond it because nothing else of yours is held.

Recent questions are capped at the last 20 and older ones fall off automatically. Typed offers and merchant reports are capped in number, and the oldest are dropped when the cap is reached. Diagnostics are a short rolling buffer.

Delete everything removes the copy under your account and your account at the sign-in service before it clears the device, so nothing of yours is kept afterwards. If you sign in with Apple or Google, they keep their own record that you used this application to sign in; ending that is a matter for their account settings, and both provide a way to revoke an application’s access.

Usage statistics you chose to send are held in our PostHog project under the random installation identifier for as long as they are useful for improving the application and for no more than two years, after which they are deleted.

Security

Your information sits in the application’s private storage, protected by the operating system’s own sandboxing and by whatever device encryption and lock screen you have enabled. On a modern iPhone or Android device with a passcode set, that is meaningful protection; on a device with no lock and no encryption, it is not, and the same is true of every application on it. The session that saves your wallet under your account sits in the phone’s keychain, under the same lock, and is readable only while the phone is unlocked.

Every network request the application makes is over HTTPS. The application declares no cleartext-traffic exception on either platform.

The most substantial security property of this application is still what it does not hold: no card number, no bank credential, and no server of our own. What exists elsewhere — the copy of your wallet under your account in Cloud Firestore, your account at Firebase Authentication, and anonymous usage counts at PostHog — is described above. The copy of your wallet says which cards you hold by name and nothing that could be used to charge one; it is readable only with a session for your account, over an encrypted connection, in a database whose access rules are published; and we do not run a server that reads it.

No system is perfectly secure. If you find a vulnerability, please report it as described in the project’s SECURITY document rather than disclosing it publicly first.

Your choices

  • Choose the way in that shares least: Sign in with Apple, with “Hide My Email”, gives us a relay address and nothing else.
  • Sign out at any time from the Account screen, which removes the provider identifier from your device and the session from the keychain.
  • Turn usage statistics off in Diagnostics, or back on.
  • Export a backup before you delete anything, if you want to keep your own copy.
  • Delete everything from inside the application, as described below.
  • Uninstall. On iOS and Android that removes the application’s private storage with it.

Deleting your information

Account → Delete everything removes all of it, in this order: the copy of your wallet under your account, your account at the sign-in service, the session in the keychain, and then everything on the device — your wallet, your offers, your history, your merchant reports, your welcome answers and your diagnostics. Each step has to succeed before the next: if the copy under your account cannot be removed, nothing on the device is touched, and the screen says why.

The sign-in service asks for a recent sign-in before it deletes an account. An email account is deleted with your password, typed on that screen; an Apple or Google account by running their sign-in sheet once more. Usage statistics you chose to send are held under a random identifier that Delete everything discards, so nothing that remains can be connected to you or to a new installation; if you want them removed as well, contact us with the installation identifier shown in Diagnostics and we will delete them.

Uninstalling the application removes everything on the device but leaves the copy under your account, which is there for the next phone; use Delete everything first if you want it gone. In the browser build, clearing site data for the application’s origin removes what the browser holds.

If you signed in, revoking this application’s access in your Apple ID or Google account settings ends that relationship on their side as well.

Your rights in the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom and Switzerland

If the GDPR or the UK GDPR applies to you, you have the rights to access your personal data, to have it corrected, to have it erased, to restrict or object to its processing, to data portability, and to withdraw consent at any time.

The unusual thing about this application is that you can exercise nearly all of them yourself, immediately, without asking us and without proving who you are — because your data is in your hands rather than ours. Access and portability are the backup export. Correction is editing your wallet or your name. Erasure is Delete everything. Withdrawing consent is signing out or turning a switch off.

For the copy of your wallet under your account we can, on request, delete it or send it to you from the database once you identify the account. For everything on the device we cannot, because we cannot see it; that is a consequence of the design and not a refusal.

You have the right to lodge a complaint with your local supervisory authority. In the United Kingdom that is the Information Commissioner’s Office; in the European Economic Area it is the authority in your country of residence.

Your rights in California

Under the California Consumer Privacy Act as amended by the California Privacy Rights Act, California residents have the rights to know, delete, correct, and opt out of the sale or sharing of personal information, and not to be discriminated against for exercising them.

For the twelve months preceding this version of the policy, and for the whole life of the application before it, the only personal information that can reach us is an email address you choose to sign in with (held by Firebase Authentication), the copy of your wallet under your account (held by Cloud Firestore) and anonymous usage counts under a random identifier (held by PostHog), each disclosed to that service provider for the business purpose of providing the service. We have not sold personal information and have not shared it for cross-context behavioural advertising, and we do not have the capability to do either.

We do not process sensitive personal information for purposes beyond those permitted without an opt-out, and we do not use or disclose it for inferring characteristics.

The right to know and the right to delete are exercised through the application itself: the backup export is your copy of everything, and Delete everything is the deletion, including the copy under your account. A request to us about that copy can be acted on once you identify the account; about anything on the device it cannot, because we hold none of it.

We honour Global Privacy Control signals by construction: there is no sale or sharing for a browser signal to opt out of.

You may designate an authorised agent to make a request, though as above there is nothing on our side for such a request to reach.

Your rights in other United States jurisdictions

Residents of Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, Texas, Oregon, Montana, and other states with comprehensive privacy laws have rights of access, correction, deletion, portability, and opt-out of targeted advertising, sale, and profiling.

Our answer is the same everywhere: we do not sell your information, we do not use it for targeted advertising, and we do not profile you; the copy of your wallet under your account is used for nothing but giving it back to you. Access and portability are the backup export; deletion is Delete everything. Neither requires a request to us and neither can be refused.

Where a law grants a right to appeal a refused request, there is no refusal to appeal: every right here is exercised from the application without asking us.

International transfers

The copy of your wallet under your account and your account at the sign-in service are stored by Google in the United States, wherever you are. Everything else stays on the device you are holding, in whatever country you are holding it.

The requests described in the network section reach Apple, Google (Firebase Authentication and Cloud Firestore), Brave or PostHog, each of which operates internationally under its own policies and transfer mechanisms; Firebase Authentication, Cloud Firestore and PostHog process what they receive in the United States. Those requests carry a store name, the fact that you are signing in and the address you sign in with, the wallet document described above, or anonymous usage counts under a random identifier.

Children

Card Genie is a tool for people who hold credit cards and is not directed at children. It is not intended for anyone under 13, and in jurisdictions where the age of digital consent is higher, not for anyone under that age.

We do not knowingly collect personal information from children, and what can reach us from anyone — an email address used to sign in, the wallet under the account, or anonymous usage counts — is described above. If you believe a child has used the application and you want their information removed, the answer is the same as for anyone: Delete everything removes it everywhere; if a child created an account and you cannot reach the phone, contact us and we will delete the account and the copy under it.

Do Not Track and Global Privacy Control

The application does not track you, so there is nothing for a Do Not Track header or a Global Privacy Control signal to change. We state this rather than staying silent because some jurisdictions require a disclosure of how such signals are handled, and “we honour them because there is nothing to honour” is the accurate answer.

Third-party services

The application can, in the circumstances described above, interact with Apple, Google (Firebase Authentication and Cloud Firestore), Brave Search and PostHog. It integrates no other third-party service. It contains no software development kit belonging to an advertiser, analytics company, attribution vendor, social network or data broker: each of those interactions is a plain request the application writes itself.

Where the application links to an issuer’s own page so you can read a card’s terms, that opens in your browser and is a normal visit to that company’s website, governed by their policy and not by this one.

Permissions

The application requests no device permissions: no location, camera, contacts, photos, microphone, calendar, notifications, health data, or Bluetooth. It requests no App Tracking Transparency permission because it does not track.

If your operating system ever shows you a permission prompt for this application other than the standard system sign-in sheet, treat it as a bug and report it.

Accuracy of card information, and what that has to do with privacy

Card earning rates are entered by hand from each issuer’s own terms page, dated, and re-checked daily by an automated process that flags pages which have changed for a person to read. Issuers reprice without notice; a rate can be wrong or out of date.

This belongs in a privacy policy because of what it implies about design: the application knows which cards you hold and nothing about what you actually spend, and it stays that way deliberately. A version that read your statements could be more accurate and would require exactly the kind of data collection this document exists to say we do not do.

Changes to this policy

This document lives in the repository beside the code it describes and is changed in the same commit as any feature that alters what the application stores or sends. An automated test fails the build if the application gains the ability to make a network request this policy does not describe.

The version at the top tells you which one you are reading. Material changes will be reflected in a new version and, where a change would alter what happens to information already on your device, the application will say so before the change takes effect.

We do not email you about changes: the sign-in service holds your address for signing in, and the application uses it for nothing else. The application itself is where a change appears.
